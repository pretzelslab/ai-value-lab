Should we put AI in customer support?

A worked reading of the support scenario: where the money is, where it balances, what each choice commits you to, and what the arithmetic cannot tell you.

All inputs assumed Defaults from models.py Threshold registered at 75%
01

The claim

One sentence. Everything below either supports it or qualifies it.

If the evidence holds

Putting AI into support is worth between $291k and $396k a year, and how much of that you actually get depends on evidence you do not yet have.

The spread is not uncertainty about the AI. It is uncertainty about how much human checking you can safely stop doing, and that is a question only measurement answers.

03

Where things balance

Three balance points. Each is a number the model computes rather than a judgement you make, and each answers a different question a reviewer will ask.

Quality neutral point 57% The control effectiveness at which you can cut review from 70 percent to 30 percent and customers see exactly as many bad answers as they do today. Below this you are buying savings with customer harm. Above it you are getting both.
Economic break even 301% The review rate at which savings reach zero. It is above 100 percent, which means review cost alone can never sink this case. You would have to review every answer three times over. The binding constraint here is quality, not money.
Your registered threshold 75% What you committed to in advance: the control must measure at least this before you would reduce review. It sits 18 points above the quality neutral point, so it is a conservative bar rather than a permissive one.
04

The line you can defend

For every review rate there is a minimum control effectiveness that keeps quality at today's level. That relationship is the automation line, and it is one equation.

05

If this, then that

What each possible measurement commits you to. Written now, before the result, so the result cannot be reinterpreted to suit.

If measured effectiveness is

Below 40 percent, with the interval's lower bound below 40

Then

Hold review at 70 percent. Take the $291k. The control is not doing enough work to justify looking at less, and pretending otherwise is how the harm arrives quietly.

If measured effectiveness is

40 to 57 percent

Then

Reduce review to 50 percent, not 30. Worth roughly $325k a year, and it holds quality at today's level. The remaining gap is an argument for a second control, not for more optimism.

If measured effectiveness is

57 to 75 percent

Then

Quality neutral at 30 percent review, worth roughly $354k. But it is below the threshold you registered, so by your own rule you pilot rather than roll out. Registering the bar only counts if you honour it when it is inconvenient.

If measured effectiveness is

Above 75 percent, lower bound above 75

Then

Your threshold is cleared. Review at 30 percent is worth $358k a year with quality improving rather than holding. Recommend proceed with the control mandatory and the review rate reviewed quarterly.

If any severity 3 failure appears

Even one, at any effectiveness

Then

The economics stop mattering. A commitment of money or a data action the policy does not permit is a hard veto. Fix the failure mode, then remeasure. This rule was registered in advance precisely so it cannot be argued away later.

If the interval is very wide

For example 45 to 85 percent

Then

You have not measured anything decisive. Sixty cases were not enough for this effect size. Report the width honestly, pilot at 50 percent review, and expand the case set before claiming a number.

06

What to actually say

The sentences. Each is defensible, each carries its own caveat, and none of them overstates.

Opening

"AI in support is worth somewhere between $291,000 and $396,000 a year. The reason it is a range and not a number is that the size depends on how much human checking we can safely stop doing, and nobody has measured that yet."

Leads with money, immediately explains the uncertainty as a knowable thing rather than a hedge.
When asked why we cannot just decide now

"Today 126 wrong answers a month reach customers. If we cut review from 70 percent to 30 percent without a working control, that becomes 294. The saving is $50,400 a year. I am not willing to recommend more than doubling customer-facing errors for $50,000 without knowing whether the control catches them."

Converts an abstract risk into a countable one. The specific number is what makes it land.
On the threshold

"We wrote down in advance that the control has to measure at least 75 percent before we reduce review. The arithmetic says 57 percent would be enough to hold quality steady. We set the bar higher deliberately, because a control we have measured once should be held to more than break even."

Shows the bar was set before the data and is more conservative than required. Both of those are credibility.
On cost, when finance asks

"Review cost alone cannot sink this. Savings stay positive until the review rate passes 301 percent, which is impossible. The constraint on this decision is quality, not money, and that is the honest way to frame it."

Pre-empts the question and moves the conversation to the real constraint.
On what could go wrong with the analysis

"The 6 percent error rate underneath all of this is an assumption, not a measurement. If it is really 12 percent, every number here moves against us. That is the first thing the experiment tests."

Naming your own weakest assumption before someone else finds it is the cheapest credibility available.
Closing

"The ask is two weeks and about twenty five dollars of API spend, to replace the two assumptions this decision rests on with measurements. Then we make the call with evidence instead of estimates."

Small, concrete, and makes the alternative sound like what it is: deciding on guesses.
07

Where the arithmetic runs out

The most important section, and the one most models leave out.

A result you should refuse

At 75 percent effectiveness, the model says you could remove human review entirely and still have fewer bad answers reaching customers than today. 105 a month against 126, worth $396k a year.

No support leader would accept that, and they would be right. When a model recommends something nobody would do, the model is missing a constraint rather than revealing an insight.

What the model does not know

All errors count the same. The model counts 105 errors against 126 and calls it an improvement. It has no idea that one wrong refund commitment can cost more than fifty wrong SLA phrasings. Severity classes exist in the protocol precisely because the economics cannot see them.

Review does more than catch errors. It trains agents, surfaces policy gaps, and builds the institutional confidence that lets anyone defend the system when it fails. Removing it entirely removes all of that, none of which appears in a minutes calculation.

Trust is not linear. A hundred small errors spread over a year is survivable. The same hundred in one week is a crisis. The model has no concept of clustering.

Some review is not optional. Contractual commitments, regulatory expectations and enterprise customer agreements can require human oversight regardless of what a measured failure rate says.

The measurement expires. Effectiveness is measured against one model version on one date. A vendor version bump silently voids it, and nothing in the arithmetic notices.

How to use this

Treat the arithmetic as a floor, not an answer. It tells you what is economically possible and where quality balances. The decision layer then applies constraints the arithmetic cannot see: severity vetoes, contractual minimums, and a sensible floor on review that no measurement is allowed to argue away.

A recommendation of "30 percent review, never below 20, with a mandatory control and quarterly remeasurement" is defensible. "Zero review, the model said so" is not, however good the arithmetic.

08

How to read any scenario

The transferable method. Six questions, in order, for any use case you assess.

  1. What is the range, and what is the range about? Never a single number. Then name the one input the spread depends on, because that input is what you go and measure. $291k to $396k, and the spread is entirely review burden
  2. Which inputs are measured and which are assumed? State this before any figure. Mark the two or three assumptions that carry the result, and be the first to point at your weakest one. error rate and control effectiveness, both assumed, both load bearing
  3. Where does it break even economically? The point at which savings vanish. If that point is implausible, say so, because it tells everyone the real constraint lies elsewhere. 301 percent review, which is impossible, so cost is not the constraint
  4. Where does it break even on quality? The point at which customers are no worse off. This is usually the number that should drive the decision and it is usually the one nobody computed. 57 percent control effectiveness at 30 percent review
  5. What did you commit to in advance? The threshold, the hard vetoes, and the analysis plan. Written before the data. This is what separates a decision from a rationalisation. 75 percent, plus any severity 3 failure is a veto
  6. What can the arithmetic not see? Severity, clustering, contractual floors, second order effects, expiry. Name them explicitly, then apply them as constraints on top of the numbers rather than hoping the numbers absorbed them. never below 20 percent review regardless of what the model says
